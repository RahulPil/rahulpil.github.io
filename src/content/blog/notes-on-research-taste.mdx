Something I've started noticing, sitting in on group meetings, is that the
people who seem to have *taste* — the ones whose questions land and whose
suggestions reroute a conversation — aren't doing anything magical. They're
just asking a smaller, sharper version of the question everyone else is asking.

I don't think you can shortcut that. It seems to come from having read enough,
been wrong enough, and watched enough projects fail in slightly different ways
that the patterns rhyme. But I've started keeping a short list of the habits
that look like the shadow of taste, even from down here:

1. Write the result you'd want before you run the experiment. If the imagined
   plot doesn't change your mind about anything, the experiment probably won't
   either.
2. When you find yourself defending a method, try to write the strongest
   version of the criticism *against* it before responding. Usually the
   criticism is partially right.
3. Notice when you're optimizing the wrong loss — when the work is moving but
   the underlying question hasn't gotten clearer.

None of this is original; most of it is folklore that anyone in research has
heard. The thing I'm trying to do is treat it as a practice rather than a
description. We'll see if anything changes.
