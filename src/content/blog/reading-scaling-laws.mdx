A year ago the Chinchilla paper felt like a settled question. *Compute-optimal* training
meant scaling parameters and tokens roughly in proportion; for a given compute budget $C$,
the loss-minimizing model size was

$$
N^* \propto C^{0.5}, \qquad D^* \propto C^{0.5}.
$$

A year later, this looks more like a useful first-order approximation than a law. A few
things have softened.

## What still holds

- **More tokens is rarely wasted.** Even at the "wrong" model size, training longer than
  Chinchilla-optimal continues to reduce loss, just less efficiently per FLOP. The
  practical implication — that for inference-constrained deployment you want a smaller
  model trained longer — has aged well.
- **The shape of the curves.** Power-law loss in $N$ and $D$, fit on a log-log plot, still
  works remarkably well across two more orders of magnitude.

## What didn't

- **The exponents move with data quality.** Mixing in higher-quality data shifts the
  effective scaling exponents in ways that the original paper, fit on a fixed mixture,
  could not see. The "law" is more accurately a *law conditional on the data distribution*.
- **Architecture matters more than was admitted.** Mixture-of-experts, sparse attention,
  and a half-dozen other improvements have each broken the curves by a fraction of a
  decimal point. Together, they make any single fit look outdated within a year.

## What I'm watching for

I think the next discontinuity is not in the loss curves but in *what the loss measures*.
Cross-entropy on next-token prediction is increasingly a proxy for something we don't have
a name for. The papers that will matter most over the next year are the ones that argue
for new loss functions — preference, verification, planning — and report scaling laws in
*those*.
