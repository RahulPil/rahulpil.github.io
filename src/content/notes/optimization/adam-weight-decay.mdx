For years, "weight decay" and "$L_2$ regularization" were used interchangeably. With SGD
that's defensible — they produce the same update:

$$
\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \nabla L(\theta_t) - \eta \lambda \theta_t.
$$

The $L_2$ penalty $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta\|^2$ contributes $\lambda \theta_t$ to the
gradient; the update from that contribution is $-\eta \lambda \theta_t$. Same thing.

With Adam, they are **not** the same thing.

## Where the equivalence breaks

Adam normalizes the gradient by a running estimate of its second moment. If you add
$\lambda \theta_t$ to the gradient as $L_2$ regularization, that *also* gets normalized.
Parameters that are already large — exactly the ones you want to decay — have large gradient
magnitudes, end up with large $v$, and get *less* effective decay than smaller parameters.

$L_2$-as-gradient-perturbation under Adam decays the *normalized* parameter, not the
parameter itself.

## The AdamW fix

[Loshchilov and Hutter (2017)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05101) proposed *decoupled* weight
decay: apply the decay term outside the adaptive update.

$$
\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \cdot \mathrm{Adam}(\nabla L, \theta_t) - \eta \lambda \theta_t.
$$

Now the decay does what it says on the tin — every parameter shrinks toward zero at the
same rate, regardless of its gradient history.

The empirical result was striking: a hyperparameter people had been tuning, somewhat
hopelessly, for years suddenly worked much better and was easier to set. The "Adam
generalizes worse than SGD" folklore softened considerably.

## A small caution

Decoupled decay is no longer "regularization" in the strict variational sense — there is no
prior on $\theta$ that this corresponds to under the usual Adam update. It is closer to a
*scheduled shrinkage*. Whether that distinction matters for your particular setup depends
on whether you care about the Bayesian story or the bottom-line validation loss.

I usually care about the validation loss.
