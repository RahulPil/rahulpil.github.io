The Kullback–Leibler divergence between two distributions $P$ and $Q$ over the same support
is

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, Q) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}\!\left[\log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\right].
$$

The compact definition hides a useful story.

## Excess code length

Suppose you're designing a prefix code for samples from $P$, but you don't know $P$ — you
*think* the distribution is $Q$. By Shannon's source coding theorem, your codewords will
be roughly $-\log_2 Q(x)$ bits long. The optimal code, had you known $P$, would have used
$-\log_2 P(x)$ bits.

The expected *excess* per symbol is

$$
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}\!\left[-\log Q(x) + \log P(x)\right] = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, Q).
$$

KL divergence is the **regret** of using the wrong model, measured in bits.

This already explains a lot:

- $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, P) = 0$: no regret if you knew the truth.
- $D_{\mathrm{KL}} \ge 0$: you can never *gain* from using the wrong code.
- $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, Q) = \infty$ if $Q(x) = 0$ where $P(x) > 0$: an event is
  cheap-to-encode by your model that the truth says actually happens — catastrophic.

## Why the direction matters

KL is not symmetric. The two directions answer different questions.

**Forward KL** $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, Q)$ asks: "Where $P$ has mass, does $Q$?"
Minimizing it pushes $Q$ to cover all of $P$ — it is *mass-covering*, and tends to produce
broad, hedged $Q$'s. This is what maximum likelihood does.

**Reverse KL** $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q \,\|\, P)$ asks: "Where $Q$ has mass, does $P$?"
Minimizing it lets $Q$ concentrate on a single mode of $P$ and ignore the rest — it is
*mode-seeking*. This is what variational inference (ELBO) does, and why VAEs sometimes
produce sharp samples from a single mode rather than the full distribution.

The choice of direction is a choice of failure mode: would you rather hedge
indiscriminately, or commit to a wrong answer with confidence?
